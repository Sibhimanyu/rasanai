Motion laws

The laws behind every RasanAI film.

RasanAI's creative engine does not guess at taste. It follows a written set of laws about story, motion, rhythm, type and image, studied from real launch films and checked by gates before a film ships. Here are 32 of them, each with a small demonstration you can watch.

Story

What a film says, in what order, and how it asks for the next step.

  • The arc

    Every launch film runs hook, turn, meet, capabilities, payoff.

  • Say what it is, and for whom, early

    Name the category and the audience before the logo, within the first quarter.

  • One message

    Say one thing; every beat serves it.

  • Claim, then proof

    Every claim is followed by the product doing it.

  • Peak and end

    People judge an experience by its peak and how it ends, so build both.

  • A clear call to action

    End on a verb and a destination, and hold it long enough to read.

  • Copy is the spine

    One thought per line, a few words, one emphasised word.

  • Benefit, not feature; specific, not vague

    Say what happens for the viewer, with something they could check.

Motion

How things move: weight, curves, timing and the habit of never standing still.

  • Purpose, and one hero

    Every move has a job, and each beat has one hero motion.

  • Natural physics

    Things ease, travel in arcs, and only overshoot when they arrive with momentum.

  • One signature curve

    Put most smooth moves on one easing curve so the film has a voice.

  • Exits are quicker than entrances

    Leave in about two thirds of the time it took to arrive.

  • Short, uneven cascades

    Stagger three to seven items inside half a second, on an irregular beat.

  • Counter-motion layers

    Let the background drift against the hero, at about a quarter of its speed.

  • Stepped for arrivals, fluid for glides

    Hold frames on discrete pop-ins; never on a smooth single-direction move.

  • Always moving, no dead frames

    Something visibly moves in every moment; a hold is composed, not frozen.

Scenes and rhythm

How scenes follow each other so the film has a pulse.

  • Scene variety

    Give every scene its own idea, layout and ground.

  • Dense, then sparse

    Follow a crowded moment with an empty one, and never run more than two alike.

  • Motivated transitions, cuts on the beat

    Every transition has a reason, and hard cuts land on the music.

  • Flash bursts land on a readable frame

    Flash one varying element, then land on a held frame that says something.

  • The ground follows the story

    Change the ground on the turn, the meet and at least one capability.

  • A distinct reveal

    The one thing you want noticed should be the one thing that differs.

Type and image

What the frame is made of: type at scale, real objects, real product.

  • Objects act on type

    Show emphasis by something physical happening to the word.

  • Display scale, with restraint

    Set the big line at display size; keep ordinary lines small.

  • Editorial still life, no generated people

    Use real-looking objects in natural light, and keep generated people out.

  • Real UI as proof

    Show the product doing its job, big enough to read.

  • Light brand

    Brand colour is everywhere; the logo is small and rare.

Rare tools and AI clichés

The moves everyone reaches for, and the budget each one gets.

  • One cursor, with a reason

    Use a travelling cursor once per film, for a real action in the product.

  • Standard entrances, once

    Word-rise, count-up and zoom-fade each get one use per film.

  • No glow pulses, no bokeh

    Never pulse a glow or float particles as atmosphere. Use real material or moving colour light.

Accessibility

The two limits no style is allowed to break.

  • No more than three flashes a second

    Never flash full-frame contrast more than three times in any second.

  • Time to read

    Hold each line for 0.6 s plus 0.3 s a word, with a minimum of 1.2 s.

Where these come from

Some laws are old and well known: Disney's twelve principles of animation, Richard Mayer's research on multimedia learning, the peak-end rule and the Von Restorff effect from Laws of UX, Apple's Human Interface Guidelines on motion, and WCAG on flashing. Others are the engine's own rules, measured from real launch films, and each page says which is which. None of them is a guarantee of a good film. They are the floor that taste then works above.

The full text lives in the open-source skill: the launch grammar, launch moves, craft, imagery and score references on GitHub.

The format is inspired by Laws of UX, which does it for interfaces. Nothing here is copied from it.
