Scenes and rhythm

Every bridge earns its place

A designed transition must carry something a plain cut would lose; otherwise cut.

Takeaways

  • Compare the designed bridge with a plain cut.
  • Carry an object, shape or velocity, or cut.
  • A fade or an empty blur is not a transition.
  • Cut during meaningful action when the join should vanish.
  • Cutting on the beat is optional, never a rule either way.

What it means

Continuity is a design option, and a hard cut is a valid choice. A bridge is worth its frames when it carries an object, a shape or a velocity from one shot to the next, or when a spatial change needs explaining. If a plain cut says the same thing, the cut is simpler to make and to revise. Cutting on the beat is a choice too: neither always nor never is a law.

In a RasanAI film

Every expensive transition has a hard-cut candidate beside it, and the film keeps the bridge only when it conveys something the cut loses. Hard cuts are welcome. A small vocabulary of two or three bridge families plus cuts is a sensible starting constraint, not an industry rule, and exceptions need a reason. Accents are assigned to particular musical elements, not automatically to every beat.

Origins and sources

Practitioners endorse both continuity and cuts in the same breath. The small-vocabulary idea is a RasanAI pilot proposal, not a studio standard.

Evidence: Single practitioner, studio practice, and research on edit blindness.

  • Sander van Dijk and Joey Korenman, School of Motion podcast: continuous motion, and cuts
  • Tim J. Smith and John M. Henderson, Edit Blindness (2008): cuts during action are often missed
  • ManvsMachine, Squarespace: a transition that enacts a product gesture
