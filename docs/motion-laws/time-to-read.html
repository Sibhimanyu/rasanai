Accessibility

Readable exposure

A line counts as readable only once it has settled, at about 2.7 to 3 words a second.

Takeaways

  • Count only settled, unoccluded time.
  • About 2.7 to 3 words a second, as a reference not a law.
  • More time for names, figures and busy pictures.
  • Keep essential messages out of fast word streams.
  • Cut words rather than speed up.

What it means

The time a layer exists on the timeline is not the time a viewer can read it: the entrance eats the first part, an exit or a cover-up eats the last. Count exposure from the moment the line is settled and unobstructed, at about 2.7 to 3 words a second, and give unfamiliar names, long figures and busy pictures extra time. If a line does not fit, cut words; speeding up the type breaks reading.

In a RasanAI film

Each essential line is measured for effective words a second over its readable exposure, with a character-rate check alongside for long technical words. Four words need roughly 1.5 seconds settled; the engine's older 0.6 s plus 0.3 s a word formula is a conservative form of the same idea. A familiar repeated word can be a graphic accent at 0.25 to 0.75 s, but that range says nothing about reading a sentence.

Origins and sources

Subtitle practice: the BBC's 160 to 180 words a minute is 2.67 to 3 words a second, with extra time for difficult content. These are adaptations from subtitling, not a kinetic-type standard, and silent-reading averages (about 238 words a minute) are not a limit for film. A "read it twice" check by the designer is a practitioner habit, not a timing law.

Evidence: Studio practice (subtitles), a study, and a practitioner check.

  • BBC Subtitle Guidelines (archived mirror)
  • Netflix, English USA Timed Text Style Guide: character-rate limits
  • Di Nocera, Ricciardi and Juola, 2018: comprehension fell at higher RSVP rates
  • Hanson Inc.: read it twice, a practitioner check (opinion)
